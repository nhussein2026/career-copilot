# Negotiation playbook — valuing, benchmarking, asking

## Total compensation — the components and their honest value

| Component | How to value it |
|---|---|
| Base salary | The only number that's certain. Everything else discounts. |
| Bonus | Target % × realistic payout — ask "what did the team actually pay out last year?"; guaranteed ≠ target. |
| RSUs (public co) | Grant value ÷ vesting years = annual value, minus volatility. Check the vesting curve — backloaded schedules (5/15/40/40) pay far less if the candidate leaves early. |
| Options (private co) | (Current preferred value − strike) × count is the *optimistic* case; illiquid until an exit that may never come. Value at a steep discount and never let options bridge a below-market base. Ask: strike, latest 409A/valuation, exercise window after leaving. |
| Signing bonus | Real cash, usually with a 12-month clawback — read the clause. Also the easiest thing for an employer to grant. |
| Benefits | Cash them out: pension/401k match %, insurance premium delta, PTO days × daily rate, remote/learning stipends. A 10-day PTO gap is real money. |
| The cliff | A 1-year cliff means year-one equity value is zero if it ends early — relevant to risk math at shaky companies. |

Compare offers on **annual total comp over the realistic tenure (2–3 years)**, not the headline.

## Benchmarking sources (state the source and its weakness)

- **Tech**: levels.fyi (best for mapped levels at known companies; thin outside big tech), Glassdoor/Indeed (noisy, self-reported, lags the market), LinkedIn salary insights.
- **Postings with bands**: pay-transparency laws (several US states, NYC; EU directive rolling out) put real bands in postings — search current postings for the same role/level/region; it's the freshest data there is.
- **Region matters more than site**: a Berlin, Istanbul, Dubai, or remote-US number are different markets; benchmark in the offer's market, and for remote roles ask what location basis the employer pays on.
- **Honesty rule**: two thin data points make a guess, not a benchmark — present it as a guess with a range, or ask the candidate to gather 2–3 peer data points (their network answers "what's fair for X at Y?" more readily than people expect).

## Leverage, calibrated

| Leverage | The honest read | The ask style |
|---|---|---|
| Competing offer in hand | Strong — and mentionable **only if real**; name the company only if comfortable | Direct: "I have an offer at X totaling Y; I'd prefer to join you if you can get closer." |
| Employed, can stay | Solid — no deadline pressure | Grounded in market data + the cost of the move. |
| Scarce skill the interviews confirmed | Real but softer | Tie the number to the specific value they saw. |
| Fresh grad / visa clock / need income now | Thin — say so | Still ask once, politely (signing bonus is the likeliest yes), and don't risk the offer with hardball. |

**Never**: invent or inflate a competing offer, imply a timeline that doesn't exist, or bluff a walk-away number the candidate won't honor. A called bluff loses the offer or starts the job on a lie.

## The ask — structure and scripts

**Order**: pick ONE primary (base if it moves; equity/signing where base bands are rigid) + one secondary fallback. Bundle everything in one conversation — never negotiate in installments.

**Email script skeleton** (call version: same beats, warmer):
> Thank you — I'm genuinely excited about <team/product, one true specific>. Before I accept, I'd like to discuss compensation. Based on <market data for role/level/region | my competing offer>, I was expecting total compensation closer to <number>. If you can get to <number on the primary>, I'm ready to sign. Is there flexibility here?

Rules that make it work: enthusiasm first and true; **one number, stated once** (whoever names a range negotiates against themselves — give a point, slightly above the target); a reason attached to the number; "ready to sign" attached to the ask (the recruiter can take a committed number to finance; a vague "can you do better?" gets a vague no); collaborative tone throughout — the recruiter is the candidate's advocate inside the process.

**The more-time script** (when the deadline is the real problem):
> I'm excited about this offer and want to make the decision well. I have <a final interview elsewhere / family logistics> in flight — could I have until <specific date, ~a week>? 
Most deadlines flex a week when asked plainly. An exploding 48-hour offer that won't flex at all is itself information about the employer.

## When NOT to negotiate

- The offer is verifiably top-of-band (public band, strong data) — ask for the secondary (signing/PTO/start date) or take the win.
- Government/union/rigid public bands — negotiate level or start date, not the band.
- The candidate said yes to a number in the process and the offer matches it — renegotiating a kept promise burns trust; the ask needs new information (a new offer, new data) to stand on.

## The mistakes list

1. Accepting (or rejecting) on the call — always: "Thank you, I'll review and come back by <date>."
2. Naming your current salary or floor when asked for expectations early — deflect to "I'd like to understand the role first; I trust you pay competitively for the level" until there's an offer.
3. Negotiating before the offer exists.
4. Five small asks in five emails — one bundled conversation.
5. An ultimatum the candidate won't keep.
6. Negotiating hard for a job they'd take anyway at any number *while other offers expire* — sequence deadlines first (more-time script), negotiate second.
7. Forgetting it ends: once the best realistic version is on the table, decide. The negotiation's purpose is a good yes/no, not a longer negotiation.
