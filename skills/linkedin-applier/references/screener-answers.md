# Screener answers — derive once, defend forever

Every answer typed into an application is a claim the candidate will be held to: recruiters re-ask the same questions on the phone screen, and offers have been withdrawn over a mis-stated year count or an authorization checkbox. So the answer bank is built **once**, from evidence, with the candidate's sign-off — and then reused verbatim across every application.

Three classes of answer, handled differently.

## Class A — derivable from evidence (compute it, show your work)

Derive from real dates in the dossier/resume, and record the arithmetic next to the number. The candidate has to be able to say the same sentence out loud in an interview.

### Years of experience

The single most-asked and most-fumbled field. There is no one number — there are two, and the honest one depends on the question:

| The form asks | Answer with |
|---|---|
| "Years of professional experience" (unqualified) | Total paid work, internships included. Round to the nearest year. |
| "…excluding internships" / "full-time professional" | Only non-internship roles. This number is usually smaller — use it when asked. |
| "Years of experience with `<technology>`" | Everywhere it was genuinely used — paid work, shipped projects, and coursework — counted from first real use, not first tutorial. |

Rules that keep it honest:
- **Count from first use to today, and don't double-count overlapping roles.** Two jobs in the same year is one year of experience.
- **Round normally.** 1 yr 9 mo → `2`. 1 yr 2 mo → `1`. Never round a 6-month exposure up to a year.
- **Per-technology counts may legitimately exceed the professional total** when the technology came from projects or study first. Note which is which in the bank, because the interviewer will ask "where did you use it?" and "a university project and two side projects" is a fine answer — being caught implying production use is not.
- **A technology with no real use is `0`.** Not "1, I've read about it." See Class C.

### The rest of Class A

- **Education** — degree, field, institution, graduation month/year exactly as on the resume. New-grad programs gate on this date; get it right.
- **Certifications** — only ones actually held, with issuer and date. An in-progress course is not a certification.
- **Languages** — use the CEFR-ish ladder the candidate can defend under a phone screen conducted in that language: Native / Professional working / Limited working / Elementary. Never inflate a workplace language; it is discovered in week one, and a "Beginner" that is honestly declared costs far fewer opportunities than one that is discovered.
- **Location and remote** — current city and country, and whether they can work in the posting's required timezone.
- **Tools/methodologies** — yes only where there is evidence. "Exposure, not competence" is a `no` on a checkbox and a sentence in the interview.

## Class B — only the candidate knows (ask, never derive)

Nothing in a resume answers these. Ask for all of them **before** the first form, in one pass, and store them in the bank:

1. **Work authorization** — "Are you legally authorized to work in `<country>`?" and, separately, "Will you now or in the future require sponsorship?" These are two different questions with two different answers, and forms ask both. Get the real status (citizenship, residence permit, work permit, its expiry) rather than a yes/no in isolation, so the answer can be adapted per country.
2. **Salary expectation** — a number or a range, with the currency and the period (gross monthly is the norm in some markets, annual in others). If the candidate doesn't have one yet, that's a real gap — route to `offer-advisor` for a benchmarked range rather than typing a guess. Whatever is entered anchors the whole negotiation.
3. **Notice period / earliest start date** — a date, not "immediately" unless it's true.
4. **Relocation** — willing or not, and to where.
5. **Work arrangement** — will they take on-site or hybrid, and within what commute.
6. **Voluntary EEO / demographic questions** — gender, race, veteran status, disability. These are optional by law in most jurisdictions. **Never answer them on the candidate's behalf and never leave a default selected**; the candidate chooses, and "Prefer not to say" is a complete answer.
7. **Contact details to expose** — phone number, whether to share it at all, preferred email.

If any Class B answer is missing when a form demands it: leave it `[unanswered]`, tell the candidate, and let them fill that one field. Do not substitute a plausible value.

## Class C — hard zeros (the skip list)

Technologies, credentials, and clearances with no evidence get `0` / `no`, permanently. The value of writing them down is that they become a **search filter**: a posting whose knockout question hits a hard zero is a Skip, decided before any time is spent on the form. Postings that gate on a hard zero are where applications go to be auto-rejected.

A hard zero is also a roadmap item — the one that blocks the most postings is worth closing (route to `career-advisor`). Re-derive the bank whenever that changes.

## Free-text screeners

Some postings replace checkboxes with a paragraph box ("Why do you want to work here?", "Describe a project you're proud of"). Draft these from real evidence, keep them to the length the box implies (3–5 sentences unless it says otherwise), and mark the draft for the candidate's edit. No invented enthusiasm for the company, no claims the resume doesn't support. When `cover-letter` is installed, use its standard.

## Maintaining the bank

Re-derive when: a role or project ends or starts (year counts move), a certification lands, authorization status changes, or a salary expectation is set. Stamp the bank with the date it was derived — a year count computed eight months ago is now wrong.
