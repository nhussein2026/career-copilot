# Surfacing questions — drawing out value the sources missed

Most candidates under-sell. The work is real; the articulation is missing. This playbook is how you recover it — by cross-referencing sources and asking targeted questions — without ever crossing into invention. A rule for every answer: if the candidate confirms it, it's usable evidence; if they hedge or can't say, it stays out or becomes a bracketed placeholder.

## How to run it

1. First, mine what's already there — cross-source gaps and vague bullets (Phase 2). Turn each into a specific question, not a generic "tell me more."
2. Ask in **one grouped batch**, organized by the categories below, so the candidate answers efficiently. Prefer concrete, answerable questions ("roughly how many users hit that service at peak?") over open-ended ones ("what were your achievements?").
3. Convert every confirmed answer into an impact bullet in Phase 4. Convert every "I think it was around…" into a bracketed placeholder the candidate can verify later.

## Categories of commonly-buried value

**Scale & scope** — the numbers that make a bullet land, and that people forget they know:
- Users / customers / MAU served; requests, transactions, or events per day/second.
- Data size (rows, GB/TB), number of services, endpoints, or models.
- Team size, and how many people the candidate led, mentored, or coordinated.
- Budget owned, revenue influenced, or cost reduced.

**Outcome & impact** — what changed *because* of the work (not just what was built):
- Performance: latency, throughput, load time, build time — before vs. after.
- Reliability: uptime, incident/bug reduction, test coverage added.
- Business: conversion, retention, revenue, cost, time saved per week.
- If no number exists, get the *direction and mechanism* ("it removed a manual step the team did daily") — still stronger than a bare task.

**Ownership & initiative** — signals of seniority that resumes flatten into "worked on":
- What did the candidate *decide* or *design*, versus implement to spec?
- Anything they started that didn't exist before (a service, a process, a tool, a doc, a standard)?
- Cross-team or cross-functional work — coordinating with design, product, data, ops.
- On-call, incidents owned end-to-end, migrations led.

**Off-resume contributions** — real work that rarely makes it onto a resume:
- Mentoring, onboarding, interviewing, tech talks, internal docs/wikis.
- Open-source contributions, plugins, or answers with real usage (verify per evidence-sources).
- Hackathon wins, side projects with real users, community/volunteer technical work.

**Recognition** — third-party validation the candidate is too modest to list:
- Promotions, spot awards, "employee of the…", positive review quotes.
- A LinkedIn recommendation already says this in someone else's words — quote its substance rather than asking again.

**Depth behind a skill** — turning a skills-list entry into demonstrated proof:
- For each headline skill: where did they use it, at what depth, to build what? A skill with a project or shipped feature behind it belongs in Experience/Projects, not just the Skills line.

## The honesty guardrails

- **Confirmed, not assumed.** A number the candidate can't stand behind becomes `[add: …]`, never a fabricated figure.
- **Their work, not the team's.** For group work, phrase the candidate's actual contribution ("built the X service within a 5-person team"), not the whole team's output as if it were solo.
- **Real depth, not inflated titles.** "Familiar with" and "expert in" are different claims — match the wording to what the evidence supports.
- **Recover, don't embellish.** The goal is to make true work visible at full strength. If making a bullet stronger requires a fact no source supports, it's a placeholder or it's cut.
